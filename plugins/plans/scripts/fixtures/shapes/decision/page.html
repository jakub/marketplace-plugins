The build cache fills its disk in eleven weeks

The cache holds 412 of 500 GB and gains 8 GB a week. Its hit rate fell from 82% to 61% on 14 October. Five decisions fix both, with no new hardware.

Hit rate

The hit rate fell from 82% to 61% in the week the cache key changed. Every project dropped, and the projects with the most commits dropped most.

On 14 October the key gained a hash of the whole source tree. Since then every commit misses, a one-line typo fix included, and the build downloads all its dependencies again.

What goes into the cache key?

The tree hash makes every commit miss. Without it the hit rate comes back. But a change to a vendored file then reuses an old cache until the lockfile changes.

Disk growth

The disk gains about 8 GB a week. At that rate it is full in the week of 25 January, and a full disk fails every build, cached or not.

Growth rose by a third in the week of the key change, because every miss uploads a new entry. Entries live 30 days, but half of them never expire. Eviction says why.

Decided. The cache stays on its local 500 GB disk. A larger disk and object storage were ruled out on 30 October: object storage adds about 40 ms to each of the 1,800 lookups a build makes.
How long does an entry live?

Thirty days keeps old branches warm, but the disk fills in eleven weeks. Fourteen days frees about 180 GB on the first night. A size cap never lets the disk fill, but in a busy week it can evict entries from the same week.

Eviction

The evictor runs on build-a and walks build-a's index. Entries that build-b writes never appear in that index, so they never expire. They hold 46% of the disk.

The evictor ran every night in October. It did its job on half the data. The 46% is counted from the index files. Whether build-b ever had an evictor of its own is unknown.

Where does eviction run?

On the cache host, one evictor sees every entry. The cache host then needs the evictor's key to the disk. On each runner, nothing new is shared, but two evictors must keep one age rule.

Write races

Three times in 30 days, both runners built one commit at once and uploaded the same key. The second upload stopped short, and the next build that read the key got a truncated archive and failed.

Nobody has made the race happen on purpose. The cause is a reading of the log, not a test.

Risk. A truncated entry fails every build that reads it until it expires, up to 30 days later. A person found all three by hand.
How do uploads avoid the race?

A temporary name and a rename cost one more call per upload, and the last complete upload wins. A lock per key stops the second upload. But a runner that crashes keeps its lock until the lock times out.

Alerts

Nothing alerts before the disk is full, and nothing alerts when the hit rate falls. Both problems on this page were found by a person reading a build log.

Which alerts go in?

Each alert pages the person on call. A disk alert at 90% gives about six weeks of warning at today's growth. A hit-rate alert also fires in a quiet holiday week.

Not changing

  • Two runners, build-a and build-b, and their build images.
  • The cache's path on each runner, /var/cache/ci, and the CI config that names it.
  • Build artifacts. They live in their own store and never in the cache.
  • The 500 GB disk, as decided under Disk growth.
